\begin{abstract}

\noindent
This paper quantifies welfare, wage, and job losses due to suboptimal infrastructure placement, as well as the compensating investments needed---via expansion and upgrading of road networks---to overcome them, for a sample of 20 Eastern Europe and Central Asia (ECA) countries. We find average welfare losses of 0.37\%, with 38.6\% attributable to prioritizing peripheral roads over inter-city connectivity. Compensating investments are more expensive if governments attempt to overcome losses through upgrading alone rather than expansion. On average, networks need to be expanded by 26.5\% or upgraded by 64.8\% to compensate welfare losses. Upgrading investments range from \$0.36 to \$23.39 billion, while expansion investments range from \$0.16 to \$19.62 billion. Misallocation is estimated to yield job and wage losses of up to 3.7\% and 2.5\%, respectively.

\section{Introduction} 

Multilateral Development Banks and governments from emerging and developing countries invest massively in infrastructure. The European Union's multiannual financial framework allocates €1.816 trillion for the period 2021-2027, with substantial portions dedicated to infrastructure development including transport networks, digital connectivity, and energy systems \citep{eu2021mff}. Infrastructure development is key to economic growth, particularly in regions with significant market inefficiencies. Recent work demonstrates that infrastructure investments can unlock productivity gains when markets are distorted: firms may be unable or unwilling to expand production even when profitable, due to spatial frictions and misallocation of resources \citep{hornbeck2024growth, cockriel2025aggregate}. \newline 

Changes in transport infrastructure---expansion and improvement of the existing network---can shift economic activity towards locations where inefficiencies are greater. This reallocation is productivity-enhancing when the value of the marginal product of inputs exceeds their marginal cost---that is, when the return-cost wedge exceeds 1. The extent of productivity gains from transport infrastructure improvements depends on the spatial dispersion of these wedges. \citet{cockriel2025aggregate} show that wedge dispersion for materials, labor, and capital is high in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, indicating substantial potential for jobs, wages, and productivity gains from expanded and upgraded infrastructure in this region.  \newline

However, to take advantage of the economic growth opportunities that infrastructure brings about, the placement of infrastructure projects should be optimal. That is, projects should be placed in locations that maximize the welfare gains from connectivity. %This, in turn, is a function of specific determinants such as the distances between any two nodes in the road network, the ruggedness of the terrain, and the existence (or not) of congestion, which is associated with the returns to scale displayed by the transport technology. 
Work by \citet{fajgelbaum2025optimal} and \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal} shows that there is scope for a more efficient allocation of public infrastructure even in advanced European countries. Misallocation of public infrastructure is not exclusively the result of bad policies. Evidence shows that history-based persistence and path dependence can play a more prominent role than geography in determining the location of public infrastructure projects \citep{allen2022persistence, graff2024spatial}. This historical persistence explains why modern transport networks often follow the trace of ancient roads or are parallel displacements of them \citep{volpe2013roads}.  \newline

We build on these works to further explore the relevance of this topic in less advanced economies, where market inefficiencies due to wedges are higher. 
Beyond calculating the welfare losses from road network inefficiencies following \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal} and \citet{graff2024spatial}, we quantify the investments needed to overcome the welfare losses from infrastructure misallocation. We also analyze what percentage of these losses are due to the prioritization of peripheral roads instead of intercity connectivity and thus explore the growth-inclusion trade-off behind infrastructure investments. Our sample covers 20 countries not included in \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal} and similar studies we are aware of. Using a reduced form solution, we also infer jobs gains in each country from these investments---a key policy priority for the World Bank's allocation of spending. Last but not least, we also model optimal investments into the road network of the entire Europe and Central Asia (ECA) region, highlighting the need to invest in upgrading of Eastern European roads to foster greater market access throughout the region. \newline %both in terms of road network expansion and upgrading,

Our results show that, under weak congestion forces, welfare losses from inefficient road network allocation range from 0.09\% (Kosovo) to 0.67\% (Estonia), with an average of 0.37\%. These inefficiencies are below those typically found in the literature (1\% to 7\%), suggesting that ECA road networks are relatively well-allocated compared to other regions. On average, 38.6\% of welfare losses are attributable to prioritizing peripheral roads over inter-city connectivity. \newline

Countries can overcome these losses through expansion and/or upgrading of the transport network. We consider two scenarios: (1) an unrestricted expansion case where links can be improved by any means (building new roads and/or upgrading existing ones), and (2) a restricted upgrading case where only upgrading of existing roads is allowed. On average, networks need to be expanded by 26.5\% or upgraded by 64.8\% to compensate welfare losses from misallocation. Assuming, based on the World Bank ROCKS database \citep{rocks2018}, that upgrading costs approximately half as much as expansion per link, compensating investments under the upgrading restriction are still 21\% more expensive on average, highlighting the economic benefits of optimal network expansions. Upgrading investments range from \$0.36 to \$23.39 billion, while expansion investments range from \$0.16 to \$19.62 billion. Misallocation is estimated to yield job and wage losses of up to 3.7\% and 2.5\%, respectively. \newline

Our paper relates to a strand of research that analyzes the welfare gains from infrastructure reallocation, expansion, and upgrading. \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal} introduce a framework to calculate welfare-maximizing transport networks and apply it to assess optimal investments and inefficiencies in the road networks of 24 European countries. They find that average welfare losses from road misallocation are 2\%, ranging between 1\% and 7\%. \citet{gorton2022trade} analyze trade network inefficiencies and optimal expansions for Latin America using a similar framework, finding average welfare gains of 1.6\% from optimal reallocation and 1\% from a 10\% optimal expansion of national road networks. \citet{graff2024spatial} study spatial inefficiency in African national road networks, finding that the region would gain 1.3\% of total welfare from better organizing its national road systems. These studies consistently find that optimal expansion or reallocation of roads reduces spatial wealth disparities. \newline

An emerging policy literature evaluates the effects of planned road interventions, with particular relevance to our ECA sample. \citet{lallleband2020} structurally estimate the spatial effects of the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) for China and Central Asia, finding that welfare will increase by 1.8\% in Kazakhstan, 8.7\% in the Kyrgyz Republic, 1.3\% in Uzbekistan, and 0.4\% in China. Within these countries, larger urban districts near trade hubs gain in welfare, while people in more distant regions tend to lose out. Barriers to internal labor mobility exacerbate wage inequalities while dampening overall welfare. \citet{cockriel2025aggregate} examine aggregate economic gains from road improvements specifically in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, finding aggregate productivity growth ranging between 0.1\% and 4.6\% across proposed policy scenarios. \newline

\section{Methodology} 


To conduct the analysis, we use a quantitative spatial general equilibrium model with multiple goods, factors, and locations following \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}. 
We solve a planner's optimization problem over the space of all possible transport networks. Thus, in this setting, the planner chooses the set of infrastructure investments to carry out in a country. Transport and trade costs are endogenous. They depend on geographic frictions, but also on the quality of infrastructure and the amount of trade/traffic along a given link---which gives rise to congestion. Following \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}, we divide geographic space by 0.5$^\circ$ grid cells and allocate population and GDP per capita using recent global estimates by \citet{LGEAW}. We then convert this grid into a graph where the nodes are the population-centroids of each cell---estimated using WorldPop 2020 data \citep{tatem2017worldpop}---and each cell connects to all 8 neighbouring cells. \newline % by aggregating smaller cells at the grid level. Then, we apportion income from the cells with GDP per capita based on population. 

For each country, we consider all national cells and cells within 100$km$ geodesic distance from national cells, alongside all connecting segments. Then, we assume that the 15 largest cells with a population above 200,000 produce different products that are traded across the network. Other cells are divided into 5 quantiles according to population, where each quantile produces its own product. Thus, in total 20 products are traded at the country level and each cell only produces and trades one of them. This is equivalent to an \citet{armington1969theory} model of differentiated products by origin, with an elasticity of substitution of $\sigma = 3.8$ based on \citet{BAJZIK2020103383}.  \newline

 The goods are produced using a linear technology $Y_i = Z_i L_i$, where $L_i$ is cell $i$'s population and $Z_i$ cell GDP per capita in 2017 PPP terms. Individual utility over traded and non-traded goods (housing) is a Cobb-Douglas function $U = c^\alpha h^{1-\alpha}$. We assume $\alpha = 0.4$ and $h = 1$, i.e., housing is proportional to population and each person is given one unit of housing based on  \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal} and \citet{graff2024spatial}, respectively. For parameterizing the model, we also follow \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal} and \citet{graff2024spatial}. 
 We set the congestion parameter $\beta = 0.13$, the returns to infrastructure $\gamma = 0.169$, reflecting increasing returns to infrastructure since $\gamma > \beta$,\footnote{This may be more realistic since \citet{shepherd2007trade}, studying trade and road infrastructure in ECA, find that a 1\% improvement in average road quality is associated with a 0.2\% to 0.6\% increase in trade, and \citet{cocsar2016domestic}, studying large-scale public investment in roads undertaken in Turkey during the 2000s, find that the distance elasticity of trade improved by 0.10–0.27 when roads were upgraded. Both studies thus suggest a value of $\gamma$ well above 0.1---\citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}'s baseline consideration based on data on major US cities from \citet{couture2018speed}. Following \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}, we instead calibrate $\gamma = \beta^2/0.1 = 0.169$, which is their increasing returns specification.} and the iceberg trade costs $\delta^\tau_{ij} = 0.116 \times \log(\text{geodesic distance in km})$. 
These parameters enter an iceberg trade costs function of the form $1+\tau^n_{ij}$, capturing the cost of trading good $n$ between cells $i$ and $j$, with $\tau^n_{ij} = \delta^\tau_{ij} (Q^n_{ij})^\beta / I_{ij}^\gamma$. Here, iceberg costs represent the fraction of goods that 'melt away' during transport, with higher values indicating greater trade frictions. The term $(Q^n_{ij})^\beta$ captures congestion effects (higher traffic increases costs), while $I_{ij}^\gamma$ captures infrastructure quality effects (better infrastructure reduces costs). %We also explore the scenario with increasing returns to infrastructure by inverting the ratio of $\beta$ and $\gamma$ to obtain $\gamma' = \beta^2/\gamma = 0.169$. 
To measure the quality of infrastructure $I_{ij}$, we use the OpenStreetMap routing engine (OSRM) \citep{luxen-vetter-2011} to compute the travel time and distance (by car) between the population centroids of neighboring cells. We derive three measures of infrastructure quality from these estimates: (1) \textit{travel speed} in km/h, (2) \textit{route efficiency (RE)}, defined as the geodesic distance between two nodes divided by the road distance, multiplied by 100, and (3) \textit{time efficiency (TE)}, defined as the geodesic distance divided by the travel time. These measures capture different aspects of infrastructure quality: travel speed reflects road quality and upgrades; route efficiency reflects network density and expansion; time efficiency combines both dimensions. We use these measures to simulate counterfactual investment scenarios: increasing speed represents 'upgrading' existing roads, increasing RE represents 'expanding' the network, and increasing TE represents an optimal combination of both. While this abstraction simplifies the spatial details of road networks, it enables comparable analysis across countries. \newline  

We define the estimated cost of building infrastructure on a link as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cost}
\delta^I_{ij} = \delta^I_0 + 0.12 \times \log(\text{Ruggedness}_{ij}) + 0.085 \times \log(\text{Population per $km^2$}_{ij}),
\end{equation}
where terrain ruggedness is taken from \citet{nunn2012ruggedness} and averaged in each cell, and population density is computed using WorldPop 2020 estimates \citep{tatem2017worldpop}. 
To obtain bilateral measures, we average the respective estimates between neighboring cells $i$ and $j$. The network building constraint is then $K = \sum_i \sum_j \delta^I_{ij} I_{ij}$, where $\delta^I_0$ is set such that the budget $K = $1,000,000. \newline

Table \ref{tab:sstat} presents summary statistics of the main variables, disregarding nodes/edges within the 100km buffer zone. Evidently, population density, productivity, and infrastructure quality differ widely across countries. The correlation of infrastructure quality (TE), with GDP per capita is 0.72 and with population is 0.44, indicating that higher income and more populated countries tend to have better infrastructure. GDP per capita and population are uncorrelated in this sample.

To conduct the analysis, we compute the optimal network using a budget $K$ equal to the cost of the existing network, and we measure the total welfare loss from the present suboptimal infrastructure network---using the comprehensive TE measure of network quality. Furthermore, we invert the model to compute the percentage change in the budget $K$ necessary to compensate these welfare losses by optimally expanding/upgrading infrastructure, i.e., for the three measures of network quality, we calculate the percentage increase in the budget---optimally allocated---necessary to increase overall welfare by the same percentage as optimal reallocation using the TE measure yields. We also calculate the share of enhancing intercity connectivity in the optimal reallocation of infrastructure to quantify over-investment in peripheral regions. For this we identify segments connecting the 15 largest cities producing own products, and then divide the (absolute) sum of the infrastructure changes on these segments, times two,\footnote{We multiply by two because the net change on central segments must come from an equivalent net change along peripheral segments, i.e., we measure the total infrastructure share involved in center $\longleftrightarrow$ periphery reallocation.} by the total infrastructure changes.   

\section{Welfare losses and investments}


We begin by calculating the welfare losses from allocative inefficiencies in public infrastructure and the investments needed to overcome them. Table \ref{tab:wloss} column (1) displays the losses. %under the assumption of congestion in transport technology, which provides a lower bound for these losses, offering a conservative scenario. The average welfare loss is of 20.45\%, with a standard deviation of 8.82\%. Losses range from a low of 4.9\% for Kosovo and up to 39.14\% for Kyrgyzstan. 
The average welfare loss is 0.37\%, with a standard deviation of 0.16\%. Losses range from a low of 0.086\% for Kosovo and up to 0.68\% for Estonia. Appendix Figure \ref{fig:Realloc} visualizes the reallocation for key countries. Link thickness represents the magnitude of infrastructure reallocation, while link color indicates whether infrastructure was added (red) or removed (blue) during the reallocation process. Node size corresponds to population, and node color indicates welfare gains from reallocation: red or orange nodes experience gains, green nodes maintain their welfare level, and blue nodes lose welfare. National nodes surrounded by a white circle or buffer nodes surrounded by a black circle produce their own unique product and are thus particularly important trading partners. \newline

The predominant reallocation pattern indicates that, to improve aggregate welfare, infrastructure is taken out of peripheral and sparsely populated regions to better connect urban centers. Column (2) of Table \ref{tab:wloss} quantifies this as the percentage of welfare losses attributable to core-periphery misallocation. It is 38.6\% on average, with a standard deviation of 16.7\%, ranging from 4.5\% in Kyrgyzstan to 70.7\% in Romania. The results thus indicate that core-periphery misallocation is substantive, accounting for a third of total misallocation, and up to two thirds in some countries. \newline


To overcome those losses, countries can expand and/or upgrade the road network. Column (3) of Table \ref{tab:wloss} displays the joint compensation scenario (combining expansion and upgrading), while columns (4) and (5) show road network expansion (increase in route efficiency) and upgrading (increase in speed), respectively, in percent, needed to overcome those losses. On average, countries have to expand their networks by a quarter to compensate the effects of allocative inefficiencies in public infrastructure. 
The compensating network expansion oscillates from 17.3\% (Montenegro) to 40.1\% (Estonia). Alternatively, countries can upgrade their network--increasing the travel speed--with the same purpose. On average, a network has to be upgraded by 65\% to overcome losses from suboptimal infrastructure placement. %This represents investments that range from (\$value) to (\$value). 
The minimum compensating upgrading is 18.7\% (Montenegro) and the maximum is 148.5\% (Uzbekistan). \newline % Investments range from a low of (\$value) to (\$value).

\section{Jobs and markdowns responses to investments}

Infrastructure investments can unlock productivity gains in inefficient markets by enabling resource reallocation to locations with higher return-cost wedges---the ratio between the value of the marginal product of inputs and their marginal cost \citep{hornbeck2024growth}. We extend this framework by exploring the jobs and wage implications of efficient infrastructure allocation. In labor markets, suboptimal infrastructure placement creates mobility restrictions, preventing workers from accessing locations where their productivity is higher. These frictions segment markets, reducing workers' outside options and increasing firms' bargaining power, leading to higher markdowns (the ratio of the value of the marginal product of labor to labor cost). \newline 

Table \ref{tab:jobs} presents job losses due to infrastructure misallocation and potential job gains from compensating expansions and upgrading. It also shows variations in markdowns---the ratio of the value of the marginal product of labor to labor cost---resulting from market integration (see Appendix for derivation). We use reduced form solutions from a simple production model where labor is the only input with decreasing returns to scale ($\alpha = 0.33$), and firms incur transport costs proportional to distance. Infrastructure quality $I_{jk}$ represents the effective distance of each link. We compute a population-weighted average across all links, where each link $(i,j)$ is weighted by the product of cell $i$ and cell $j$'s populations, yielding aggregate infrastructure quality $I$. The reduced form solutions for changes in markdowns and jobs are given by Equations \ref{eq:dmd} and \ref{eq:djobs}, respectively.

% \todo[inline]{Changes in markdowns (monopolistic competition).}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dmd}
\frac{\Delta \eta}{\eta}=\frac{-\gamma \tau}{1+\tau} \frac{\Delta I}{I}, % -\frac{1}{I-1}\frac{\Delta I}{I}
\end{equation} 
where $\tau = 0.5/I^\gamma$ roughly follows \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}, assuming fixed aggregate flows ($Q = 1$), and baseline iceberg parameter $\delta^\tau = 0.5$, as further detailed in the Appendix.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:djobs}
\frac{\Delta L}{L}=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha} \frac{\Delta \eta}{\eta}.
\end{equation}

Table \ref{tab:jobs} shows that optimal reallocation of existing infrastructure yields a 33\% increase in population-weighted infrastructure quality on average, ranging from 7.2\% (Kosovo) to 82.2\% (Greece). \section{Optimal Investments in ECA's Transport Network}

Beyond national network inefficiencies, we also consider the transport network of the entire ECA macro-region and simulate optimal investments into it. To do this, we first select the largest cities with a population above 250,000 that are at least 50$km$ apart, using data from the Simplemaps World Cities Database \citep{simplemaps}. This yields 281 key cities. We then use OSRM to obtain fastest car routes between them, and intersect and process their geometries following \citet{krantz2024optimal} to obtain an undirected graph representation of the continental road network comprising 952 nodes and 1722 edges. We assign to each node city populations within 30$km$ of that node, and calibrate its productivity as the inverse-distance weighted GDP per capita in 2017 PPP terms across all cell-centroids within 50$km$ of the node using the 0.25$^\circ$ data by \citet{LGEAW}. We also reparameterize each edge with the road distance and travel time from OSRM. Furthermore, we apply a network finding algorithm following \citet{krantz2024optimal} to identify high-potential new/missing links and verify that they can be constructed. This yields 274 potential links, highlighted in green in Figure \ref{fig:ECAnet}, which would decrease road distance by at least 50\% along the nodes they connect.

Consequently, roads crossing it are not part of the existing network, but the network finding algorithm identified several links reconnecting Russia to Ukraine. Since much physical infrastructure was destroyed in the war, these links may need to be rebuilt, although at a lower cost than new roads. \newline

\begin{equation} \label{eq:COST}
\ln\left(\frac{\text{cost}}{km}\right) = \ln(X) -0.11 (\text{dist} > 50km) + 0.12 \ln(\text{rugg}) + 0.085 \ln\left(\frac{\text{pop}}{km^2}\right),
\end{equation}
where $X = $100,000 for new links and lower for different types of upgrades following \citet{krantz2024optimal} and information in the ROCKS database \citep{rocks2018}. Appendix Figure \ref{fig:ECAcalibdet} visualizes various network calibration details discussed above. The network in Western Europe is substantially better and faster than in Eastern Europe, Russia, and Central Asia. 


\subsection{Optimal Investments in Partial Equilibrium}

To analyze the cost-benefit of building/upgrading each link, we simulate the total gains in market access (MA) from upgrading each link, divided by the cost of upgrading it. The MA of each node is computed as the inverse-travel time weighted sum of GDP of all other nodes, and total MA is the simple sum of MA across all nodes. Total MA is thus denominated in dollars per minute of travel time, and the gains per investment are denominated in dollars per minute per dollar invested. \newline 

Figure \ref{fig:ECAMAPG} shows the return in dollars per minute per dollar invested from building/upgrading each link individually to allow a travel speed of 100$km/h$, while keeping all other links at their original speed. This is a partial equilibrium result, as all other links are kept fixed. Re-establishing connectivity along the Russia-Ukraine border yields the highest marginal returns, but upgrading major transport routes, especially to major metropoles like Moscow and Istanbul, as well as to Western European cities like Rotterdam and Paris, also yields high marginal returns.

\subsection{Optimal Investments in General Equilibrium}

We also consider the optimal general equilibrium spending of a fixed budget on extending and upgrading the network using the quantitative framework of \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}. According to our cost estimates, the entire network admits investments of 74.1 billion 2015 USD, of which 51.6 billion fall on upgrading existing links and 22.5 billion on constructing the 274 proposed links. \newline 

We run simulations with investment budgets of \$10 and \$25 billion to create informative resource allocation problems. In terms of model parameterization, we follow the gridded approach above except that infrastructure $I_{jk}$ is given in terms of speed ($km/h$) instead of time efficiency, and bounded between the current speed and 100$km/h$. As in \citet{krantz2024optimal}, the estimated per-$km/h$ infrastructure building cost $\delta^I_{jk}$ is the estimated cost of upgrading a link (length times cost/km given in Appendix Figure \ref{fig:ECAcalibdet}) divided by the difference of $I_{jk}$ from 100$km/h$, assuming that investing the estimated amount will bring a link to 100$km/h$. Another difference from the gridded approach are the values of $\gamma$ and $\beta$, which, due to the larger network size and numerical issues with lower parameter values, are set to $\beta = 1$ and $\gamma = 1.2$, reflecting the more likely increasing returns case for large networks. Setting $\beta = 1$ is crucial to achieve convergence. These values are close to \citet{graff2024spatial}, who sets $\gamma = 0.946$ and $\beta = 1.177$. \newline 

The productivity calibration follows \citet{armington1969theory} and \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}: we let 25 key cities produce different products and divide the remaining cities into 4 quartiles by population. Thus, in total there are 29 products traded across the network differentiated by origin. Like \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}, we assume that cross-goods congestion applies. \newline 


Figure \ref{fig:ECAGE10B} shows the optimal \$10 billion investment allocation. In Western Europe, some funds are spent on building selected new links, whereas in Eastern Europe a combination of new links and upgrades is chosen. Notably, all links connecting Russia to Ukraine are restored, and a large new road connecting St. Petersburg in Russia to Tallinn in Estonia is built. Very little infrastructure is built in Central Asia; only a few links on the east side of the Caspian Sea are upgraded. This is likely due to Central Asia's sparse population and low productivity. % \newline

\section{Summary and Conclusion}


This paper examines the extent of spatial misallocation of road infrastructure in 20 Eastern Europe and Central Asia (ECA) countries using a welfare-maximization framework in a neoclassical general-equilibrium spatial model following \citet{fajgelbaum2020optimal}. We find that misallocation follows a dominant spatial pattern where too much is invested in connecting rural/remote communities at the expense of improved inter-city connectivity. The average welfare loss is 0.37\%, with 38.6\% attributable to misallocation between urban centers and the periphery. Although welfare losses are modest, they require considerable investments to remedy: an average 26.5\% network expansion (which includes upgrading) or 64.8\% network upgrading is needed to compensate welfare losses from misallocation. \newline

We also estimate that misallocation led to 1.4\% fewer jobs on average, and implementing compensating investments would increase jobs by 2.4\% if done through network expansion and 1.5\% if done only through upgrading. \newline

These findings suggest that mistakes have been made in the spatial prioritization of road network investments in the ECA region, and getting investments right from the start is highly beneficial. Spatial economic models considering aggregate welfare, employment, and productivity should be taken into account---alongside social equity, environmental, and other considerations---when planning or allocating funds to major infrastructure projects.
